Part 3 — Tips inside lessons (written in simple English)

#LessonTipCheckCorrection / comment
1How to PractiseIn Russian schools, students often learn English by reading and translating. That builds good grammar and vocabulary — you already have that. What is usually missing is listening to real speech and speaking out loud a lot . Focus your time there.☐ OK
☐ Fix
2Consonant SoundsEnglish /p t k/ need a puff of air — Russian п, т, к have none, so pin can sound like bin . /ŋ/ ( sing ) is new: don’t add a g or k at the end. And don’t make final consonants voiceless — see Final Consonants .☐ OK
☐ Fix
3TH: think and thisRussian speakers often replace TH with s / z (“I sink so” for I think so ), or with t / d (“tree” for three ), or f / v . Each one can change the meaning. Look in a mirror: if you cannot see your tongue, it is not TH.☐ OK
☐ Fix
4W vs VRussian has no W. Round your lips like a short у and keep your teeth away from your lip: w = “у-э”, not в . Words with wh ( what, where, when ) start with W too.☐ OK
☐ Fix
5English H and RRussian х scrapes in the back of the mouth. If you use it, “hello” sounds harsh: “khello.” Practise by sighing: “haaa.” Then say “ha-llo” with that same soft breath.☐ OK
☐ Fix
6The Canadian TThe flap T is almost the same as the Russian р tapped once, very quickly! Say вода fast, then try “wa- t er” with a single quick tap. But do not roll it.☐ OK
☐ Fix
7Final Consonants: bag / backIn Russian, voiced consonants at the end of a word become voiceless ( дуб → [дуп]). In English they don’t: bag ≠ back , eyes ≠ ice . Make the vowel longer before a voiced consonant.☐ OK
☐ Fix
8Short and Long Vowels: ship / sheepRussian и sits between these two English sounds. If you use и for both, “live” and “leave” sound the same. Make the short /ɪ/ more relaxed and lower — think of the vowel in Russian был or мыло , but with the tongue a bit more forward.☐ OK
☐ Fix
9cat / cut / betRussian speakers often say /æ/ as э , so bad sounds like bed , and man sounds like men . Open your jaw more for /æ/ — imagine you are about to bite a big apple.☐ OK
☐ Fix
10Word StressRussian also has free word stress, and it changes meaning: му́ка (torment) vs мука́ (flour), за́мок vs замо́к . You already know the feeling. The difficulty is that you must learn the stress of each English word, just like in Russian.☐ OK
☐ Fix
11The Schwa /ə/In Russian, unstressed о is not pronounced as “o”: молоко sounds like [mə-la-KO], and хорошо like [khə-ra-SHO]. English does the same thing, just more often. Do not pronounce every vowel the way it is spelled.☐ OK
☐ Fix
12Sentence Stress: Content vs Function WordsRussian speakers often give every word the same weight: “I — WANT — TO — GO — TO — THE — STORE.” It sounds very careful and slow. Practise making the small words almost disappear.☐ OK
☐ Fix
13Rhythm: English Keeps the BeatRussian rhythm also reduces unstressed syllables, but English does it more strongly and more regularly. A common result is speech that sounds “staccato” — each word separate. Practise with a beat: clap, tap your foot, or use music.☐ OK
☐ Fix
14Focus Stress: Changing the MeaningRussian often shows focus by changing word order . English mostly keeps the word order and uses voice stress instead. If you do not use focus stress, people may miss your point — or think you are not interested.☐ OK
☐ Fix
15Linking Words TogetherRussian speakers often put a small stop (a glottal catch) before words that start with a vowel: “pick | it | up.” This makes speech sound choppy. Try to slide the last consonant into the next word. Practise slowly first: “pi... ki... tup.”☐ OK
☐ Fix
16Falling Intonation ↘Wh-questions are easy: Russian Где ты живёшь? also falls. The challenge is that English falls are often bigger — the voice starts higher before it drops. A small, flat fall can sound bored or tired to English listeners.☐ OK
☐ Fix
17Rising Intonation ↗A Russian yes/no question has a sharp rise-and-fall on the key word : Ты лю́бишь хоккей? If you use that pattern in English, it can sound surprised, suspicious or annoyed — “ DO you like hockey?!” In English, keep the voice going up at the end and stay up.☐ OK
☐ Fix
18Sounding Polite and FriendlyNeutral Russian intonation is flatter than English. Russians are sometimes described as “serious” or “cold” by Canadians — not because of what they say, but because of how the voice moves . This is not about personality; it is just a different melody. Try to use a bigger, warmer voice movement than feels natural at first.☐ OK
☐ Fix
19Articles: a, an, the — or nothingAsk: could I say “какой-то / один” in Russian? → use a . Could I say “этот / тот самый”? → use the . Am I talking about everything of this kind? → no article.☐ OK
☐ Fix
20Present: Simple vs ContinuousWatch the -s with he/she/it: She work s . And state verbs ( know, like, want ) are not used with -ing.☐ OK
☐ Fix
21Present Perfect vs Past SimpleIn Russian: Я живу здесь два месяца — present tense. In English this becomes present perfect : I live here two months. I am living here for two months. I’ve lived here for two months. / I’ve been living here for two months.☐ OK
☐ Fix
22Talking About the FutureRussian Я буду… is often translated as will every time. But for plans, Canadians almost always say gonna : “What are you gonna do this weekend?” — “I’m gonna sleep!” Using will for every future sounds formal.☐ OK
☐ Fix
23Words That Go TogetherCommon translation traps: “make a photo” (делать фото) → take a photo ; “give a question” (задать вопрос) → ask a question ; “do sport” → play sports / work out ; “in the internet” → on the internet ; “I have 30 years” → I’m 30 ; “visit a lesson” → go to class .☐ OK
☐ Fix
24Small Talk & GreetingsIn Russia, “Как дела?” is often a real question, and people may answer honestly with details. In Canada, a long answer about problems to a cashier or a classmate you just met will surprise them. Save real answers for friends and family.☐ OK
☐ Fix
25Keeping the Conversation GoingMany Russian speakers fill silences with э-э-э or ну… . English listeners notice this immediately. Train yourself to say um , so and well instead.☐ OK
☐ Fix
26Opinions, Agreeing & DisagreeingRussian communication is often more direct, and that is a sign of honesty. In Canada, the same directness can sound rude or angry — even when you don’t mean it that way. Adding a softener (“I think,” “maybe,” “kind of,” “I’m not sure”) costs nothing and helps a lot.☐ OK
